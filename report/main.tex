\begin{abstract}
This report describes the Hack2Publish entry to the IMU Odometry Challenge. We train one network, with a single
set of weights, that takes raw 6-axis IMU at 200\,Hz and outputs the mean body-frame velocity of every 1\,s window
for all four platforms. The main idea is to let the network see much more than one window: at inference we cut
each test trajectory into 16\,s chunks, predict a dense 20\,Hz velocity for the whole chunk, and average the
overlapping chunk predictions. The network is a strided convolutional stem, eight dilated temporal-convolution
blocks and a small three-layer transformer (3.9\,M parameters); a set of hand-computed strap-down features is added
to the stem output. Training samples platforms and trajectories the way the metric averages them, and adds a loss on
the integrated velocity error so that the ATE$_{20}$ term is optimised as well as the AVE term. Augmentation is kept
physically exact: random re-mounting of the sensor (applied to the IMU and to the velocity label), time dilation
using the gravity vector available at training time, and bias/scale/noise. Only the challenge data was used. We chose
every hyper-parameter on the released \texttt{val} split and then refit the final model on train+val for 160 epochs
(EMA plus weight averaging over the last 20 epochs). On the official scoring service the submitted model reaches a
TartanIMU Score of 0.219 over the 89 test sequences (macro AVE 0.180\,m/s, ATE$_{20}$ 0.563\,m). On the public
leaderboard it scores 0.28609, ninth place at the close. We also document two properties of the drone data that we
believe limit every IMU-only entry, and a measured leaderboard noise of $\pm$0.01--0.02 between identical recipes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Three things make a general inertial odometer difficult. First, the physics is only partly observable: a 6-axis
IMU measures specific force and angular rate, so gravity, sensor biases and the mounting of the sensor are mixed into
the same six numbers as the motion we want, and slow motion (a hovering drone, a person standing still) hardly shows
up at all. Second, the four embodiments in this challenge differ by an order of magnitude in speed, vibration and
dynamics, and the test set does not say which is which, so the network has to work that out from the signal. Third,
the ground truth was produced by different capture systems, and, as we found during the challenge, they are not
equally consistent with the IMU streams.

Our approach was shaped by one measurement we made early on: the per-window velocity is strongly correlated between
neighbouring windows (lag-1 correlation 0.85 for the car, 0.78 for the dog, 0.56 for the human), and an oracle that
simply averages the true velocities of the two neighbouring windows already halves the all-zero error. A single
second of IMU cannot estimate gyroscope bias, low-frequency tilt or the embodiment with any confidence; sixteen
seconds can. Since the test trajectories are provided whole, we built the entire solution around trajectory context.
The other two ingredients are a training procedure that mirrors how the score is averaged, and augmentation that
respects the physics of the sensor. What we think distinguishes the entry is less any single component than the
discipline of selecting everything on the held-out split and then explaining, with measurements, where the remaining
error comes from. Our contributions are (i) the context model and training recipe, (ii) an experimental record of
what helped and what did not, and (iii) two data findings about the drone split (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}) that we hope
are useful to the organisers.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Overall Architecture}
The pipeline is drawn in Fig.~\ref{fig:method_overview}. The input is a chunk of $T{=}16$ consecutive windows, i.e.\
$3200\times 6$ raw samples $[a_x,a_y,a_z,\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z]$ with gravity retained. We do not subtract a
mean; each channel is only divided by a fixed constant ($3\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ for the accelerometer,
$0.4\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ for the gyroscope). Keeping the mean matters twice: gravity is the only tilt cue the network
has, and a zero-mean rotation augmentation stays exact.

A two-layer strided convolution (kernel 9, stride 2; kernel 11, stride 5) turns the 200\,Hz signal into 20\,Hz
tokens of width 192. In parallel we compute, without learned parameters, a small set of strap-down features on the
same chunk: the gyroscope is integrated into a relative orientation for every token (a parallel prefix product of
per-token rotation matrices), the accelerometer is rotated into the frame of the chunk start, its chunk mean is used
as a gravity estimate, and the de-gravitated acceleration and its running integral are rotated back into the current
body frame. Because one of the drone sources has an inverted gyroscope $z$ axis (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), we compute
these features for both signs of $\omega_z$ and let the network decide; a $1\times1$ projection adds the 18 channels
to the tokens. Eight residual blocks follow, each a depthwise convolution (kernel 5, dilations 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 1,
2) and a pointwise MLP with GroupNorm, giving a receptive field of roughly 13\,s. A three-layer transformer encoder
(4 heads, learned positional embedding, pre-norm) then mixes the 320 tokens of the chunk, and a linear head outputs a
velocity per token, i.e.\ a dense 20\,Hz velocity. The per-window prediction is the mean of the 20 tokens inside the
window. An attention-pooled descriptor also feeds a 4-way platform classifier, but this head only exists in the
training graph; it is never evaluated at inference and nothing downstream depends on it. There is no platform input,
no routing and no per-platform component anywhere in the network.

The choices made with cross-platform generalisation in mind were: no mean normalisation (see above), bidirectional
context (bias and tilt are effectively estimated over 16\,s rather than 1\,s), and sampling that gives every platform
the same number of gradient steps regardless of how many hours of data it has.

\subsection{Data Processing}
Windows are used exactly as the organisers define them (window $k$ is \texttt{imu[200k:200k+200]}). Whole
trajectories are kept in memory and a training sample is a random 16-window chunk of one trajectory; trajectories
shorter than 16 windows are padded by repeating the last sample and the padded windows are masked out of the loss.
The 20\,Hz targets are 10-sample means of \texttt{vel\_body}. No filtering, resampling, bias correction or
calibration is applied, at training or at test time.

Augmentation is applied on the GPU, per chunk. (a) \emph{Re-mounting the sensor}: a random rotation (uniform axis,
angle uniform in $[0, 15^\circ]$, or $[0, 45^\circ]$ for drone chunks) is applied to the accelerometer, the
gyroscope and the velocity label alike, which is exactly what a differently mounted sensor would have measured.
(b) \emph{Time dilation}: the chunk is resampled by a factor $s\sim\log U(1/1.3, 1.3)$; the velocity label and the
gyroscope are scaled by $s$, and the dynamic part of the acceleration by $s^2$, while the gravity part, which we
know from the training quaternion, is kept. This produces realistic faster and slower versions of every flight.
(c) Sensor errors: accelerometer scale $1+\mathcal N(0,0.02)$ and bias $\mathcal N(0, 0.15\,\mathrm{m/s^2})$,
gyroscope scale $1+\mathcal N(0,0.02)$ and bias $\mathcal N(0, 0.02\,\mathrm{rad/s})$, white noise of
$0.05\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $0.004\,\mathrm{rad/s}$.

\subsection{Training and Optimization}
The loss is
$L = L_{\mathrm{win}} + 0.5\,L_{\mathrm{dense}} + 0.2\,L_{\mathrm{drift}} + 0.05\,L_{\mathrm{plat}}$.
$L_{\mathrm{win}}$ is a Huber loss ($\beta = 0.25$\,m/s) on the Euclidean norm of the per-window velocity error,
which is the AVE up to the Huber knee; $L_{\mathrm{dense}}$ is the same loss on the 20\,Hz output;
$L_{\mathrm{drift}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\lVert\sum_{k\le t}(\hat v_k - v_k)\rVert / \sqrt{t}$ penalises the
accumulated body-frame error inside the chunk, which is the kind of correlated bias that ATE$_{20}$ measures and a
per-window loss does not see; $L_{\mathrm{plat}}$ is the cross-entropy of the auxiliary platform head. The weights
were set by hand at the start and never tuned.

We use AdamW ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.99$, weight decay 0.02), a OneCycle schedule with peak learning rate
$1.5\times10^{-3}$ and 8\,\% warm-up, batches of 64 chunks, 250 optimiser steps per epoch and gradient clipping at
2.0. An exponential moving average of the weights (decay 0.998) is kept during training, and the final weights are
the plain average of the EMA weights taken at the end of each of the last 20 epochs. Sampling: a platform is drawn
uniformly, then a trajectory uniformly within that platform, then a random chunk position. Selection runs lasted 60
epochs; the final refit 160 epochs. Training is a single stage; there is no curriculum, no fine-tuning, no domain
adaptation and no platform-specific specialisation. The final checkpoint is simply the averaged weights at the end
of the fixed schedule -- with \texttt{val} inside the training set there is nothing to select on, and we did not
want to select on the leaderboard (Sec.~\ref{sec:selection}). Selection runs were trained on an Apple M5 laptop GPU
(about 2--3\,min per epoch); the ranked checkpoint was trained by a Kaggle script kernel on a Tesla T4 in 3.6\,h.

\subsection{Inference}
For each test trajectory we read only the \texttt{imu} array and the window order. Chunks of 16 windows are slid
over the trajectory with a stride of 2 windows; every chunk is passed through the network once; the per-window
outputs of overlapping chunks are averaged with a raised-cosine weight (plus a small floor) so that a window is
trusted most from the chunk in which it lies near the centre. Trajectories shorter than 16 windows are padded by
repeating the last sample and the padding is discarded afterwards. Trajectories are never concatenated. There is no
test-time augmentation, no ensembling, no scaling, clipping, smoothing or calibration, and no test-time adaptation.
The method is offline: it uses past and future samples of the same trajectory.

\section{Development Experiments}
\label{sec:devexp}
Unless stated otherwise, the numbers in this section are TartanIMU scores on the held-out \texttt{val} split (80
trajectories, organisers' scorer) of models trained on \texttt{train} only. Fig.~\ref{fig:val_curves} shows the
training curves of the main runs.

\emph{Architectures.} We started from the per-window 1-D ResNet of the public starter kernel, which reaches about
0.30 on val. Our first context model (width 128, 2-layer transformer, 16\,s chunks, 30 epochs) reached 0.2247; that
single change is by far the largest gain we saw. A wider trunk with 32\,s chunks was worse at the same number of
epochs (0.2293), so 16\,s stayed. A bidirectional GRU in place of the transformer gave the same accuracy but was three
times slower on our hardware. Widening the trunk to 192 channels and three transformer layers, at 60 epochs, improved
val from 0.2030 to 0.1987; the improvement was spread evenly over the platforms.

\emph{Schedule length.} The same recipe trained for 60 instead of 30 epochs went from 0.2233 to 0.2030 (drone-B AVE
0.328$\to$0.271\,m/s, car 0.129$\to$0.117, human 0.085$\to$0.073). On the public leaderboard the train+val refits
went 0.3625 (30 epochs), 0.3466 (60), 0.2975 (100), 0.2870 (160); a 240-epoch run of the wide model did not improve
further (0.2897).

\emph{Sampling.} Drawing trajectories uniformly within a platform instead of in proportion to their length took val
from 0.2030 to 0.1982. The metric averages per trajectory, so a 13-window drone clip counts as much as a 35-minute
car drive; length-proportional sampling had been under-training exactly the short clips that carry most of the error.
Raising the drone share of the sampling to 40\,\% (0.2013) or adding a vibration-amplitude augmentation (0.2065) was
worse than plain uniform sampling. The ranked entry combines uniform sampling with the wider trunk; we did not have time to validate
that combination as a separate 60-epoch run, so its only scores are those of the train+val refit itself (0.2861 public,
0.219 full test).

\emph{Drone-targeted augmentation.} Rotations up to $45^\circ$ for drone chunks, a fourfold oversampling of the
racing-drone source and time dilation together moved val from 0.2247 to 0.2233, but the racing-drone trajectories
themselves did not move at all (AVE 0.674$\to$0.676\,m/s). That was the first hint that their error is not a matter
of training signal.

\emph{Things that did not work.} The strap-down features on their own gave no measurable gain: integrating the raw
gyroscope over 16\,s drifts by 5--20\,m/s because of uncompensated bias, so the network's own short-horizon
integration is already better (we kept the features because they cost nothing). A learned per-chunk time-shift
head, supervised with IMU-to-ground-truth offsets we measured on the training set, predicted the offsets of unseen
recordings well (for example $-66$ and $-48$\,ms predicted against $-40$ and $-70$\,ms measured) but did not improve
the score. Rotation test-time augmentation (0.2246 against 0.2247), a 3-window moving average (ATE worse on every
platform) and a denser overlap at inference (identical) changed nothing. Averaging the predictions of two to four
runs improved val to 0.2171 and the public score to 0.354 from 0.363, but that is not a single weight set and none of
our ranked entries uses it.

Overall, more epochs and better sampling helped every platform; nothing we tried helped one platform at the expense
of another; and model scale helped a little on val but did not show on the leaderboard.

\subsection{Per-Sequence Results}
Table~\ref{tab:sequence_results} in the appendix lists all 89 sequences. The pattern is a concentration rather than a
spread. The car, human and dog sequences are uniform: their AVE sits within a narrow band whatever the length or
speed of the sequence. Among the drone sequences, the ones that belong to the racing-drone source (short, 3--7\,m/s,
each flight recorded by two IMUs, see Sec.~\ref{sec:data}) have an AVE several times that of any other sequence,
and a handful of them account for most of the drone term. At the other end, the slowest hovering drone sequences
have an AVE about equal to the standard deviation of their own ground-truth velocity, which is what one gets by
predicting the mean: hover drift of less than 0.2\,m/s is simply not visible in the IMU.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
Section~\ref{sec:devexp} is our ablation record. Ordered by effect on val: trajectory context ($-0.08$), schedule
length ($-0.02$), uniform trajectory sampling ($-0.005$), model scale ($-0.004$), drone augmentation ($-0.001$);
strap-down features, the time-shift head, test-time augmentation and smoothing gave zero or negative changes.

\subsection{Generalization Analysis}
\label{sec:data}
The held-out val split predicted the test set well: 0.198 on val against 0.219 on the full test set. The public
split is a different matter, 0.286, and the organisers' baseline shows the same gap (0.538 full test, 0.637
public). Everything about this gap is in the drone data. Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} plots the per-trajectory val error
of our recipe against the mean speed of the trajectory. Car, dog and human sit at 0.07--0.13\,m/s regardless of
speed. Drone-B, the second drone source, sits at 0.15--0.4\,m/s, and its error is actually lower at 3--4\,m/s than
when hovering. Drone-A, the racing drone, sits at 0.45--1.0\,m/s at every speed.

We spent a good part of the last week trying to understand drone-A and found two things, both measured on the
released training and validation files.

\emph{Frame conventions.} Drone-B's gyroscope $z$ axis is inverted with respect to the body frame of its quaternion
and velocity: the ground-truth yaw rate equals $-\omega_z$ with $R^2 = 0.97$. Its accelerometer does not match the
ground-truth specific force under any relabelling of the axes either (the $R^2$ at 2\,Hz is negative; an
unconstrained $3\times3$ fit gives axis gains between 0.6 and 2.1). The other three platforms and drone-A have
identity conventions with $R^2$ between 0.97 and 0.99. The network copes with drone-B because the source is easy to
recognise from the signal (its high-frequency accelerometer content is ten times lower), but whatever it learns about
physics on drone-B's 3.4 hours does not carry over to drone-A, which has about 30 minutes of data.

\emph{The racing-drone recordings.} Every fast flight of drone-A appears twice, recorded by two different IMUs, one
level and one pitched by about $45^\circ$ (e.g.\ \texttt{drone\_val\_0005} and \texttt{0006}). Cross-correlating
$|\omega|$ with the angular rate derived from the quaternions shows that the two IMU streams are offset from the
ground truth by different amounts, about $-10$\,ms for one IMU type and $-40$ to $-70$\,ms for the other. After
aligning the two recordings in time and fitting the best rotation between them, their velocity labels still disagree
by 0.15--0.9\,m/s, which corresponds to roughly $5^\circ$ of inconsistency in the mounting at 4--7\,m/s. No model
that sees only the IMU can do better than that disagreement on this source, and about a quarter of the test drone
trajectories are of this type. Several dog trajectories also have offsets of 40--150\,ms, but at dog speeds these
cost little.

We saw no negative transfer between platforms. Every improvement of the shared recipe improved every platform, and
removing one platform from training never helped another.

\subsection{Failure-Mode Analysis}
The failures we can name are these. Racing-drone flights: errors of 0.5--1\,m/s that look like a bias per
recording, consistent with the timing and mounting inconsistencies above. Slow hover: a velocity oscillation of
$\pm 0.15$\,m/s at 0.1\,Hz produces about $0.1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ of acceleration underneath 4--6\,$\mathrm{m/s^2}$ of
vibration, and the network predicts the mean. Fast cars: the 25 val windows above 3\,m/s have errors of 3--4\,m/s,
because nothing in the training data drives that fast. Two things remain open: a principled way to bring drone-B's
sensor convention in line with the rest of the data (it is not a simple relabelling of axes, so we could not
canonicalise it), and any use of the fact that each racing flight is recorded twice.

\section{Insights and Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:insights}
Is a single universal inertial model feasible? For the part of the problem that is observable, yes, and without any
specialisation. One 3.9\,M-parameter network with no platform input gets 0.05--0.07\,m/s AVE on three platforms and
around 0.27\,m/s on ordinary drone flight, it identifies the embodiment on its own, and every change that helped one
platform helped the rest. We never found a reason to add a platform-specific component.

Where is the bottleneck? Not in the model. The single largest remaining error comes from one drone source whose
labels are inconsistent with each other at the level of tens of milliseconds and a few degrees. More capacity, more
epochs and more augmentation left that error where it was while everything else kept improving. The second largest
remaining error, hover drift, is a limit of the sensor, not of the learning.

What transfers? Reading whole trajectories, sampling the way the metric averages, and augmentation that respects
the sensor physics do not depend on any particular IMU or vehicle and should carry over to other platforms and
datasets. Anything the network has learned about a particular source's axis convention or timing offset will not,
and in a platform-blind test set with recognisable sources that kind of knowledge is rewarded. Per hour of data,
the clean and slow drone-B recordings gave far more than the fast and noisy racing-drone recordings: the drone-B
error kept falling with training, the racing-drone error did not move.

About the evaluation: with 45 drone sequences averaged per trajectory, a public split is dominated by a few short
racing flights. We measured differences of $\pm$0.01--0.02 on the public leaderboard between runs of the same
recipe on different GPUs, which is larger than most of the differences we were trying to detect; val, with all its
windows, was a much better instrument. Directions we would pursue: architectures that estimate bias, gravity and
time offset explicitly and with an uncertainty; self-supervised consistency between the two IMUs of the same
flight; and, above all, training data whose synchronisation has been verified.

\subsection{Feedback to the Organizers}
The rules, the metric and the starter kit were clear, and shipping the exact scorer was the single most useful
thing in the kit: our local val floor (0.013) and all-zero score (1.015) matched the published references, so we
trusted val throughout. What cost the most time was discovering the drone data properties of Sec.~\ref{sec:data}
ourselves; a note on per-source conventions, IMU types and synchronisation quality (or a fix of the drone-B
gyroscope sign and the racing-drone offsets) would have let us spend that time on the model. The metric behaved as
documented, but per-trajectory averaging makes a 13-window drone clip worth as much as a 35-minute drive, and that is
what makes the public leaderboard noisy; a per-window average within a platform, or a larger public split, would
help. We would also suggest either merging or declaring the second-IMU duplicates of the racing flights. Finally,
because the sources are recognisable from the signal, a platform-blind test set measures source recognition as much
as generalisation; a genuinely held-out platform, or a causal evaluation track, would measure the latter more
directly.

\section{Conclusion}
A context network over whole trajectories, trained with a sampler that mirrors the metric and with physically exact
augmentation, gives a single unified inertial velocity estimator with one set of weights and no post-processing. It
scores 0.219 on the full test set and 0.286 on the harder public split, up from our own starting point of 0.553.
What remains is dominated by one drone source whose ground truth disagrees with itself; closing that gap needs
better data, or models that estimate synchronisation and mounting explicitly.
